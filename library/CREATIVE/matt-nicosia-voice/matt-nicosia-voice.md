# Matt Nicosia's Voice

Built from 18 real LinkedIn posts pulled directly from his profile (matt-nicosia-b2278732), July 2026. This is not a vibe description, it's a technique inventory. Every technique below is named so drafts can be critiqued at that resolution ("this needs a flat-statement open" beats "doesn't sound like him").

Corpus source: `/Users/mattnicosia/bldg-labs/matt-linkedin-corpus.md` (18 posts, raw text). Re-pull periodically and re-derive techniques as new posts accumulate; this doc is a snapshot, not a ceiling.

## Design principle

**Asymmetry over symmetry.** Default to structural imbalance. Avoid sentence pairs that mirror each other, paragraphs that resolve neatly, predictable rhetorical balance, or any pattern where the second half echoes the shape of the first. Symmetry reads as engineered. Asymmetry reads as thought. This sits above the individual technique bans -- it is the reason contrast-pivots, negation stacks, and parallel constructions are all banned. When in doubt, break the pattern rather than completing it.

## Named techniques

**1. Flat-statement open.** Never opens with a question or a hook-y setup. States a claim as settled fact in sentence one. "Most of these AI for construction companies are going to be dead in 24 months." "Procore Technologies is a dead saas walking." No ramp, no "let's talk about."

**2. No-ramp problem naming.** States the pain in one or two short sentences before any solution appears. "Roofers miss calls all day because every single one of those missed calls is just a job walking over to the next guy on Google." Problem first, always, exactly as the brand voice notes already say.

**3. "And here's what X still doesn't get" pivot.** A specific rhetorical hinge that turns from problem to indictment of an incumbent. Also appears as "And it's not even their fault" (empathy pivot) and "Here's what's hard to fake" (reveal pivot).

**4. Us vs. them framing, but "us" means builders/estimators, "them" means software incumbents.** Procore, Autodesk, "software suits," RSMeans. Never customers, never AI itself, always legacy platforms and legacy pricing models. The contrarian target is specific companies/institutions, not vague "the industry."

**5. Historical analogy as clincher, not opener.** "Netflix didn't warn Blockbuster either." "This is netflix vs blockbuster." Always short, always dropped near the end as a mic-drop line, never explained further. Trusts the reader to get it.

**6. Real numbers over vague claims.** "38% of contractors now report AI actually making a measurable financial impact." "758 real construction proposals." "4 hours on a takeoff that should've taken 30 minutes." Specificity substitutes for hype.

**7. Short declarative sentence bursts, especially for lists.** "Click. Enter. Tab. Save. Repeat. 400 times." "Concrete. Steel. Framing. Mechanical. Electrical. Finishes. Sitework." Staccato lists replace comma-separated prose when cataloging pain points or scope.

**8. One-line gut-punch closer, often unrelated in form to the paragraph above it.** "Every extra click is a tax. And taxes are a crime." "Proof beats a resume every time." These land as aphorisms, not summaries.

**9. First-person builder credibility, stated plainly, no humility hedge.** "I'm an estimator. I run an estimating firm. I'm constrained by the estimator shortage. And I sit on the other side of the table as a GC." Short subject-verb clauses stacked in a row to establish standing before making a claim.

**10. Direct CTA, low-friction, no corporate phrasing.** "Comment TAKEOFF or DM me." "Comment or send me a message." "matt@bldglabs.ai" typed as plain text, not a formatted link. Never "reach out to learn more" or "let's connect."

**11. Concession before the claim ("I'll admit/I know").** "The Procore guys will smirk at a post like this." He names the counterargument or the skeptic's reaction before dismissing it, doesn't pretend no one disagrees.

**12. "I built this because I got sick of X" origin framing.** Product posts almost always root the build decision in a personal irritation or witnessed injustice ("watching good contractors lose to faster ones"), not a market-sizing rationale.

**13. Rhetorical question used sparingly, only to invite engagement, never to open a post.** "Agree or Disagree?" "Do you see any GCs on the rise right now..." Always near the end, always inviting a comment, never used for suspense.

**14. Technical nomenclature dropped inline without translation.** "quantity takeoffs," "value engineering," "RS Means," "Bluebeam," "ROM," "MEP," "GWB," "R-13 insulation." He does not explain these to a lay reader. The audience is assumed to be construction-fluent. Do not soften or define jargon in drafts, it signals outsider voice.

**15. Present-participle scene-setting for narrative posts.** "You open a set of plans you've never seen before... You carry the entire project in your head. You price it. You defend it." Second-person immersive walkthroughs used to dignify a role (estimators, roofers) before pitching the product.

## Sentence rhythm

- Short-short-long is common: two clipped sentences, then one longer sentence that carries the actual insight.
- Paragraphs are almost always 2-5 sentences. No walls of text. Line breaks do a lot of work; a one-line paragraph for emphasis is normal.
- Contractions used throughout ("it's," "isn't," "I'm"). Never stiff.
- "You know" filler does NOT appear in written posts (it's a spoken habit per user profile notes) -- written voice is tighter than spoken voice. Do not force "you know" into LinkedIn/X copy; save that register for video/voice content.

## Structural pattern for product/announcement posts

1. Flat-statement problem (1-2 sentences)
2. Empathy or context beat (why this isn't the person's fault / why it's hard)
3. "So I built X" turn
4. What it actually does, in plain mechanical terms (no feature-speak)
5. What makes it different from competitors (never named competitors get a full paragraph, gets one sharp contrast line)
6. Personal stake / origin motivation
7. Low-friction CTA

## Structural pattern for opinion/hot-take posts

1. Flat-statement claim, controversial, stated as settled
2. One supporting paragraph naming the incumbent/institution by name
3. Concession naming the skeptic's reaction
4. Historical analogy as closer

## X-specific format: "translate the news for a 16-year-old" (new, July 2026)

X cadence target: 5 posts/day, high volume, real-time reaction to what's cutting edge that day (new model releases, AI news, industry shifts). The core move on X: translate AI/tech news for a construction audience that isn't AI-fluent, using plain, teenager-simple language for the AI/tech concept itself.

Critical distinction, do not confuse these two directions of translation:
- Construction jargon (RS Means, GWB, takeoffs, MEP, value engineering): NEVER simplified. The audience is construction-fluent. Keep exactly as-is per technique 14.
- AI/tech jargon (agents, context windows, tokens, model names, inference cost): SIMPLIFY hard. Explain like the reader is 16 and has never touched this stuff. This is the new X-specific move: strip AI concepts down to "here's what this actually means for your business" in one or two plain sentences before or instead of the technical term.

Pattern for these posts:
1. What happened. Flat-statement lead, one sentence, no hedging, name the specific model/version/release.
2. Best way to use it. Two acceptable forms, pick based on how much the news warrants:
   - Single move: one direct infinitive sentence, one concrete actionable step tied to a contractor's actual day.
   - Multiple moves: "Best use cases for this:" (or similar direct lead-in) followed by a short bullet list, each bullet a concrete action, not a feature description. Use this form when the release supports more than one real use case, it fills out the post without adding filler prose.

No bridge sentence explaining significance in between the two steps, ever, in either form.

Optional closer for X news posts: instead of an offer to demo/show something running on a specific tool or workflow (risk: commits to a proof point that may not exist, e.g. claiming a specific model when you haven't actually used it), close with an open engagement question inviting replies. Pattern: "Here's where I could see this being powerful:" + a use case, then "How are you guys using it?" or similar direct question. Never promise to show a live demo of a named tool/model unless it's something you've actually run. Keep the use case in this closer trade-neutral too, no "on the jobsite" or other construction-only scene-setting, same audience-scope rule as the bullet list below.

Audience scope check: default use-case language to a general GC/estimator/builder audience, not a single trade. "Before you're off the roof" only works for roofer-specific content (e.g. BLDG Capture posts). For general X news posts, use trade-neutral phrasing: missed calls, bid follow-ups, proposal drafts, inbox, site walk, jobsite, subcontractor coordination. Don't anchor a broad-audience post to one narrow trade's daily routine.

Bullet-list ordering rule: when using the bullet form, the FIRST bullet must be a general/broad-audience use case (applies to almost any business owner, not construction-specific) to widen the post's reach beyond a construction-only audience. Remaining bullets stay construction/contractor-specific as usual.

Do not add a labeled "translation" step or any structural signposting ("here's what that means:"). The simplification has to read as just how Matt naturally explains it, not as a formatted breakdown.

This is an extension of the existing Post 4 pattern in the corpus (Claude Sonnet 5 / GPT-5.6 post) -- that post already does this instinctively. Lean harder into the plain-English translation step, that's the gap to widen.

## Corrections log (patch immediately when Matt corrects a draft)

- **Quoted dialogue keeps the exhaustion.** When someone says why they need help, they don't say "we're under-resourced." They say "none of us have a second to breath." Keep the raw human fatigue in quotes. Don't sanitize dialogue into business-speak.
- **Name the actual humans, not the process.** "By my staff" not "tested internally." "My crew" not "field-validated." "My team" not "quality assured." The people are the proof.
- **Preempt skepticism with everyday language.** "Not a funnel" is how a normal person says "this is not a lead generation tactic." Don't use marketing-adjacent words for disclaimers.
- **CTA register varies by offer type.** Sales/conversion ask: "DM me" or plain email. Research/conversation ask: softer -- "please reach out," "no strings attached."
- **No transition words between paragraphs.** Don't open a paragraph with "Now," "So," "And," or "But" as glue from the previous one. Each paragraph lands on its own.
- **Staccato bursts are for lists, not scene-setting.** "Scheduling. Estimating. Documents." doesn't work inside a story paragraph. The staccato technique (technique #7) is for cataloging pain or scope ("Concrete. Steel. Framing."), not for describing what you were showing someone on a call. In narrative, use real sentences.
- **"I built a free tool" is banned phrasing.** Just say "I built X" directly, no "tool" as a filler noun. E.g. "I built a free comp report," not "I built a free tool [that shows/for] a comp report."
- **"I run a GC" is factually wrong, never write it.** When referring to the company, name it explicitly first: "At Montana Contracting Corp (my GC firm)..." or "Montana Contracting Corp (my GC business)". The parenthetical clarifies without making the firm name the only reference. Avoid standalone "my GC" or "I run a GC".
- **Never drop the subject pronoun to sound punchy.** "Pulled this from BLS" is wrong, must be "I pulled this from BLS." Matt always keeps "I" as the explicit subject when he's the one doing the action, does not clip it for a staccato effect. This applies broadly: any sentence describing something Matt personally did (pulled data, built something, ran a firm) keeps the "I," full stop.
- **No insulting-adjacent framing toward the reader.** Don't tell the audience what they don't know in a way that reads condescending, e.g. "most of you have no idea where you actually sit in that range" was rejected as insulting. State the fact plainly ("most people don't know...") and let the reader draw their own conclusion, don't frame it as a personal gap in *their* knowledge specifically.
- **No timeline-framing in announcements.** "Spent the last few weeks building" and "Just got it live" are corporate-agency register. Matt announces things by stating what exists now, not by narrating the build timeline. "We just launched a new website" not "I spent the last few weeks building a website." The work speaks; the timeline doesn't matter.
- **Conversational register: "I" and "we" throughout, no polish.** Every sentence should read like Matt telling a friend what happened, not like a press release. When the draft sounds like it could appear on a company blog, it's wrong. "I designed and built it myself, which was a fun challenge" is right. "This project represents a departure from conventional contractor web design" is wrong.

## Banned (per brand law + observed absence in corpus)

- The "same X, smaller Y" collapse. A two-noun-phrase parallel construction with no verb, built on symmetry instead of real content ("same brain, smaller bill," "same tool, smaller price tag"). This is a generic AI-writing tic, not a Matt technique. Never use it. If a contrast needs to land, write a real sentence instead.
- Parallel contrast constructions ("not X, Y" / "you don't have X, you have Y" / "X isn't X, X is Y"). Symmetric negation-replacement patterns. These sound clever but read as mechanical AI prose, not human voice. Examples Matt flagged: "you don't have a product, you have a clock," "the moat isn't the moat anymore, the moat is free." Never use them. Do real contrast with real sentences, not parallel symmetry.
- Negation-stack reveal ("No pitch. No deck. Someone who runs a real company asking for help."). Stacking two or more negations ("No X. No Y.") to build drama before a positive reveal. This is a mechanical AI tic, same family as contrast-pivot. Matt flagged it directly. If the real thing is "someone asked for help," just say that. Don't negate what it wasn't first.
- Repetitive grammatical skeletons across consecutive sentences. Three sentences in a row that share the same grammatical shape -- Subject-Verb-Object, Subject-Verb-Object, Subject-Verb-Object -- with different words but identical structure. This is a default AI prose pattern. Vary sentence openings and internal grammar between consecutive sentences, not just sentence length. Matt does this instinctively (short-short-long) but the ban makes it explicit: no two adjacent sentences should share the same grammatical skeleton.
- Vague product references. Name the actual model/version/thing, never "a version of X." If the news names a specific release, use that name in the post.
- Loose transitional filler in use-case lines ("let it blah," "and just," open-ended trailing off). Use direct infinitive construction instead: "The best way to use this is to [specific action]." Concrete verb, concrete object, stop.
- Explaining why something matters. Never write a sentence whose job is to justify significance ("that matters because...", "this is a big deal since..."). State the fact, trust the reader, move straight to the actionable step. No justification bridge.
- "That's not nothing." Banned outright, cliché hedge-praise phrase, appears nowhere in the real corpus.
- Meta-posting about the content system itself. Posts about "building in public," "content systems," "AI-slop avoidance," AMBER/radar, dry-hour honesty, or process theater about how we write posts are founder theater (and were explicitly killed when framed as content-OS ship logs). Matt's correction: these posts contain the AI-slop qualities AMBER was built to avoid. Prefer construction, money, product, labor, distill twists -- not the content machine.

## Reply / comment register (X under someone else's post)

Separate from main-feed flat-claim posts. Matt wants down-to-earth conversational replies for growth, not punchy talking-at-people cadence.

Do:
- "Here's what I'm seeing..." / "We've been running into..." / "On our side..."
- Soft personal takes owned as opinion, not universal law
- Hermes/stack language when the thread is about agents (real interest)
- Speed-to-lead / estimating / GC detail when the thread is ops
- End clean. No forced bait question unless the whole point is a genuine ask

Do not:
- Lead with a main-feed style hot open under someone else's post
- Sound like a coach grading them
- "Thoughts?" / "Agree?" spam ends
- Pitch product, drop links, or list credentials beyond one light standing line


- Em-dashes / en-dashes (brand law, hard rule)
- Corporate hedge phrases: "reach out to learn more," "let's connect," "excited to announce," "thrilled to share" (note: "thrilled" appears once in the corpus for a hire announcement, personal congratulations register only, not product marketing)
- Explaining construction jargon to a general audience
- Opening with a question
- Vague stats ("many contractors," "a lot of GCs") when a real number is available
- "Move the needle," "contrast-pivot," other banned corporate phrases already in memory
- "Straight" as an honesty intensifier. Don't write "tell you straight," "say it straight," "straight answer." It reads as tough-guy filler, not real voice. Say what happens plainly: "every sub tells you whether they're bidding" instead of "tells you straight whether they're bidding." (Flagged 2026-08-15.)

## Using this skill

When drafting: pick the structural pattern that matches the post type, use at least 3-4 named techniques explicitly, then self-check against the banned list before presenting a draft. When Matt corrects a draft, patch this skill with the specific technique that was missed or mis-applied, don't just fix the one post.

## Quality gates (quantitative)

Run these checks against any draft before presenting it:

- At least roughly one-third of sentences begin with a concrete subject (a person, a company, a thing) rather than a pronoun, expletive ("it," "there"), or adverb.
- No paragraph contains more than one sentence exceeding roughly 35 words.
- Consecutive sentences differ noticeably in length. The short-short-long rhythm is natural; same-length runs are mechanical.
- When read aloud, the writing sounds like speech, not like prose optimized for the page.

Failing any single gate is not a hard kill. Failing three or more in the same draft means the draft needs structural rework, not line edits.
